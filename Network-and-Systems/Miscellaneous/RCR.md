## Research Misconduct

Includes:
- Fabrication
- Falsification
- Plagiarism

For short FFP.
**Research Misconduct is defined as the FFP in proposing, performing or reviewing research records.**

Here *research record* is defined as the record of data resulting from scientific inquiry, and includes both physical and electronic versions of:
- research proposals
- lab records
- progress reports
- abstracts
- thesis
- oral presentation
- internal reports 
- journal articles

### Fabrication
Making up data or results and recording/reporting them.

Example:
- Creating fake data to support a hypothesis
- Creating fictional graphs

### Falsification

Manipulating data to fit a certain graph or to support a trend, and make look more convincing than they actually are.

For example, reusing certain data to illustrate a point, or to remove certain data that challenges such a notion.

### Plagiarism

Using someone else's work or results without reference to them or attribution. Using language used by other writers if done too much may also be considered plagiarism.

## Why Research Misconduct Happens

### Individual Factors

Stress and personality traits (like narcissisms, arrogance or self-deception).

Time pressure may also play a role (like an approaching deadline, or tenure raise for a long time researcher).

### Organizational Factors

Lack of ethical climate or not forcing rules may lead to research misconduct on an organizational level.

Also, poor mentoring can also play a role.

### Related Factors

For the most part, data is very easily available due to technology and the internet (be legal, or illegal). These factors play a big role in causing more "temptation" towards research misconduct.

### Others

#### Questionable Research Practices

Intentionally preventing certain things from challenging a result (like not reporting negative or false-positive results). Premature publication of research before all of it's aspects has been considered or deliberately using statistical tests that are not suitable to certain use-cases may also be considered in this category.

These are behaviors that are "really" close to being a misconduct, but some may wrongly argue that they are not, because they are taking definitions too literally.

These are also called **Detrimental Research Practices**.

#### Noncompliance

Deliberately disregarding professional practices or not adhering to federal and corporate law.

#### Sexual Harassment

Self explanatory.

#### Authorship Disputes

By itself, it is not misconduct, but if FFP is involved, it certainly is.


## Effects of misconduct

### On Society

Public trust is lost, and it suffers as a whole. What also does not help, is that these conducts are really hard to identify and may cause a lot of problems given this long time.

Imagine promoting a medicine to be safer than it's actually is.

### On Trainees, Colleagues and Organization

For example:
- Students whose advisor is guilty of this, might need to delay their studies and find a new advisor
- Organization can be discredited.
- Research teams may be denied future research grant due to a tarnished reputation.

## How to prevent it

There are policies and research practices that can help, though things like cultural background may cause these policies to not be equally useful for different teams.

## Detecting Misconduct

- Online tools to check for plagiarism.
- The community itself, as they are always in direct contact with a research when such a misconduct can happen. Seniors in particular are the most important when it comes to fact checking results.

## How To Report It

There are official processes for reporting such things, though it is very much recommended to consult with knowledgeable individuals before proceeding with such an allegation.

There are 3 phases involved:

### Inquiry

Is it true? Is there an actual need for investigation?

### investigation

Formal development of a record and investigation of the research, which may lead to a positive possibility of research misconduct.

Investigation may also lead to the result that suggests the allegations were just a misunderstanding and nothing else.

The process is done by a specialized committee

### Adjudication

Recommendations are reviewed and action is taken.

These actions may be:
- A warning
- Suggestion to undergo training
- Report of federal crime or fraud
- Return of granted funds at the behest of the federal government

## Data Management

Scientific methods rely on a hypothesis that must then be proved to be consistent with data given an experiment.
The data and the results are then controlled by experienced professionals to gauge how much the results hold ground.

**KEY:** The study must have enough data that allows it to be *reproduceable* by others.

### Case Studies
- Don't share information outside of you research circle without the permission of the supervisor. If they are working on a patent, it is EXCEPTIONALLY important.
- It is the responsibility of the data collector to make sure that the data isn't compromised. 

## Creating Good Data

One of the main things that a good set of data must possess, is **operationalized concepts**. This is done by defining concepts in such a way that it shows:

- **Reliability**
- **Validity**

### Reliability

Allow for repeated measurements, showing that the conditions that these measurements were done in have been correctly identified and controlled

### Validity

In this context, it means whether or not we are *sure* we are reporting the data that we *think* we are reporting, given our set of instruments.

This means that we have confidence that the measured data has proven correlation to the quantity we want to check, or at least there is strong evidence of it.

### How To Achieve Them?

- Be a learned individual in you discipline.
- Based on your profession, these requirements may be looser or stricter. And it is important to understand that it is paramount that you know these requirements, always.

## Data Management Categories

### Methodological
Well-designed in terms of statistics. These include:
- Sampling techniques
- Confidence intervals
- Distribution management
- Data presentation

### Technological
Data lifecycle management. These include assuring that the data remains:
- Fresh and available to the users
- Remain confidential
- Assures integrity

#### Confidentiality
Preventing inappropriate users from accessing the information, protecting the privacy of the researchers and user, and protecting the intellectual property related to the research.

#### Integrity
Recording and preserving data correctly, so that it remains usable and stable.

#### Availability
All appropriate users have access to the data when needed. This may also extend to the ones who wish to reproduce the research.

This part may include federal regulations and standard procedures based on the field in question.

### Ethical, Legal and Professional Policies

The requirements that the organization that oversees the research may induce on the data collection procedure. These include things like privacy policies, federal regulations and government constraints on measurement tools.
